The KDS Box

Deployed

A plug-in kitchen display system that runs on a Raspberry Pi. No cloud, no subscription, no IT setup.

Lead engineer · Sky Zone Gaithersburg · March 2026 to present

3x
Orders completed in the same time
About 9 min
Wait time, down from 10 to 20 min
All staff
Adopted by the whole kitchen

The box runs its own private Wi-Fi network and serves a React app to any tablet with a browser. Orders move between screens instantly over Socket.IO, and everything lives in a local SQLite database, so service keeps going even when the internet doesn't. It replaced verbal order calls in Sky Zone's busy café kitchen, where the staff use it on tablets.

  • Three screens: order entry with per-item notes, a kitchen display with color-coded urgency timers, and a party-order board with shorthand codes and automatic duplicate merging.
  • A REST API handles orders, history, and party orders, and Socket.IO events like orders:new and party:completed keep every screen in sync.
  • Built to keep running: it's a systemd service with auto-start and crash recovery, SQLite uses write-ahead logging for crash resilience on the Pi's SD card, and on startup the server reads the highest saved order number so a reboot never reuses one. I've physically tested it for recovery after a power loss.
  • Android tablets couldn't reliably resolve the kds.local hostname, so I added a travel router with a DHCP reservation to give the Pi a fixed address.
  • Next up: moving the menu out of per-tablet storage and into SQLite behind an API, so any venue can set up its own menu without touching code.

Carousel of Creation

Completed

Montgomery College SET Design Challenge · Fall 2025 · Two-person team with Nahom Tadesse

2nd place
2025 SET Design Challenge
Under $200
Total build budget

A kinetic sculpture that tells the story of the creative process. Seven laser-cut panels (The Spark, Curiosity, Shaping, Struggle, Breakthrough, Building, and Transformation) sit inside a 3D printed carousel, and an Arduino turns it one chapter at a time.

  • I owned the electronics: an Elegoo Mega driving a 28BYJ-48 stepper motor through a ULN2003 driver, with Next and Reset buttons, status LEDs, and an IR remote.
  • The code moves the carousel in exact 90° steps across four chapter positions, debounces every press, stops it from over-rotating, and sends it home on reset.
  • To use a generic IR remote, I wrote a small scanner sketch that printed each button's code over serial, then mapped those codes to next, previous, jump to a scene, and reset.
  • We designed the parts in CAD, printed them in PLA, and laser-cut the story panels at the MC Innovation Center.
  • Our first plan had LCD screens and a branching storyline. We scaled it back to something we could build well, then fixed fit problems by resizing and reprinting parts.

RFID Clock-In System

Completed

Co-leader · Montgomery College, ENEE 150 · Fall 2025 · Team of three

A badge-tap time clock for shift work. A Raspberry Pi reads RFID badges through an RC522 module over SPI, gives each new badge a label like User 1 or User 2, and shows on a 20x4 character LCD (LCD2004) how long that person worked and what they earned at a set hourly rate. I programmed the Python side: badge authentication, UID parsing, GPIO control, timestamp logging, and pay calculation. It all lives in a 3D printed enclosure a teammate designed in CREO, with the screen on top and the reader below.

  • Our first screen, a 16x2 LCD, only ever showed random characters. We rechecked the wiring, moved the contrast pin to a potentiometer, and added a 330 Ω resistor on the backlight, but nothing fixed it, even with our instructors helping. A 20x4 LCD with the same pinout worked right away, so the first module was faulty.
  • The Pi ran through a VS Code remote connection with no internet access, so pip couldn't install anything. We downloaded the RPLCD and mfrc522 packages from PyPI on a laptop and copied them straight onto the Pi.
  • The LCD runs in 4-bit mode to save GPIO pins, a reset button on an interrupt clears every open session, and repeat reads from a badge left on the reader get ignored.
  • The enclosure took two prints. The first one's LCD window was too tight, so it was redesigned with better tolerances and a hollower shell, and the RFID window was drilled and sanded to fit.

Neural Networks and GANs

In progress

University of Maryland · ENEE 301 · Fall 2026

97.3%
Best accuracy on 10,000 test digits
26 runs
Across 9 network settings
3 of 4
Of my own hand-drawn digits read right

A three-layer network that reads handwritten digits, written in Python with NumPy and no machine learning framework. It learns by backpropagation on all 60,000 MNIST training images, then gets scored on 10,000 images it has never seen.

Accuracy can swing by about a point between runs just from the random starting weights, so I trained each setting more than once and compared averages. More epochs helped more than anything else. Too high a learning rate (0.6) hurt badly, about four points lower, and growing the hidden layer past 200 nodes barely moved the needle. The best setup reached 97.3%.

Then I tested it on digits I drew myself in Paint. At the better settings it read my 2, 3, and 5 correctly every time, but it kept calling my 8 a 3. Close the left side of a 3 and you get an 8, so the network was going with the shapes it was most sure about. My digits were drawn with a mouse instead of a pen, and that small difference was enough to trip it up. That taught me more than the 97% did: doing well on a test set isn't the same as handling new, real input.

Two side projects I took as far as a prototype. Neither shipped, but both taught me about scoping a project and picking the right tool for it.

Quoridor in Unity

Paused

Quoridor is one of my favorite board games, and I think it's seriously underrated. You race your pawn to the other side of the board while placing walls to slow your opponent down, but you can never fully block their path. I wanted to give it the hype it deserves, so I started building a digital version in Unity.

I got a lot of it running: a 9x9 board generated from code, pawns that highlight their legal moves, and horizontal and vertical walls with live placement previews, all in 3D. But building the mechanics from scratch turned out to be harder than it looked. Rules like legal wall placement and always leaving a path to the goal are easy to explain and surprisingly tricky to code, and I set the project aside before it was finished. It's still one I want to come back to.

Sky Zone Party Host Assigner

Working demo

At Sky Zone, a manager assigns a party host to every party customers book. With more than 20 parties in just two days, picking the right host for each one without double-booking anyone was a lot for one person. So I started building a Unity app for it: enter the party, enter your list of hosts, click one button, and it assigns someone.

It looked random, but it wasn't. The app weighed each host's existing parties, their availability, and a few other factors to pick the best fit with no time clashes. I got it to a working demo with screens for adding hosts and parties, a host roster, and a parties list showing who got assigned where. Then I stopped. The logic was getting complicated, I realized building every screen from scratch in Unity wasn't the way to go, and the park solved the problem by giving party assignments to another person.
